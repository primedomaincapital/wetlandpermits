Wetland permitting, mitigation banking, and wetland loss, by the numbers

Section 404 permit volumes, mitigation bank counts and credit prices, nationwide permit reissuance, and wetland loss figures — from published sources.

Three different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures the gate: how much ground the Corps permits each year and how the permit works. The second kind measures the market that the gate creates: the banks, the credits, and the prices developers pay to offset what they fill. The third kind measures what is at stake: how much wetland the country still has and how fast it is losing it. The first kind tells a reader how much work the system handles. The second tells a reader what that work is worth. The third tells a reader why the gate exists at all.

What the latest numbers show is a system under strain from both directions: permit volumes remain enormous — more than 73,000 acres of aquatic resources covered by Corps permits in a single fiscal year — while the resource being permitted is shrinking, with net wetland loss accelerating by more than 50 percent between 2009 and 2019, and the market built to offset the loss is consolidating into the hands of a few large operators whose credits can cost more than $100,000 each.

How to read these numbers

Included: GAO's fiscal year 2023 permit acreage and credit-price range; the Corps' nationwide permit counts and the January 2026 reissuance; the Fish and Wildlife Service's 2009–2019 wetland extent and loss figures; RIBITS registry counts and credit-sale totals as mirrored by MitiVision in November 2026; published in-lieu fee rates, which are administrative list prices rather than bank transaction prices; state program figures from Florida, Iowa, West Virginia, and Wisconsin; and the Sackett decision and the rulemaking that followed it. Excluded: any single "average price per mitigation credit," since no bank publishes prices and the federal registry has no price field — the figures shown are published in-lieu fee rates and reported ranges, not market averages; and any claim about how many acres a credit represents, since credit units differ by state, method, and habitat type.

Permit acreage covers permanent and temporary impacts together and is not a measure of wetland loss alone. RIBITS credit sales are ledger withdrawals counted as transactions, not quantities of credits, and credit classes are not additive across wetland, stream, and species types. The global market size is a research firm's modeled estimate, labeled as such. Forecasts are labeled as forecasts, not actuals.
