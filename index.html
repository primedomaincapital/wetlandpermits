The thesis

A name for the gate every project must pass through.

Nothing gets built on wet ground in America without a piece of paper from the Army Corps of Engineers. Section 404 of the Clean Water Act bars the discharge of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States without a permit, and the Corps' 38 district offices are the ones that issue them. The work around that permit — delineation, avoidance and minimization, state water-quality certification under Section 401, compensatory mitigation — is a high-fee professional service sold by national firms as a lead offering.

The demand is structural, not cyclical. The Fish and Wildlife Service counted 116.4 million acres of wetlands in the conterminous United States in 2019, less than 6 percent of the land area, and the country was losing them faster than before: a net loss of 221,000 acres between 2009 and 2019, with vegetated wetlands down 670,000 acres over the same period. Development keeps pressing into the remaining ground, and every project that touches it needs the same sequence of work — a delineation to find the wetland, a permit to fill it, and credits to replace it.

Exact, and built for a reachable buyer pool. The buyers are nameable: the environmental consultancies that lead with wetland services — W3, raSmith, S&ME and their peers — the mitigation bankers who sell credits by the acre, and the civil engineers and developers whose projects cannot start without the permit. They all search the same two words. This domain is those two words, in order, with nothing added.
