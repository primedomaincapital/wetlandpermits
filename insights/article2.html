Wetlands, Sold by the Credit

America has 3,344 mitigation banks, and a single credit can cost more than $100,000. The market that sells wetlands by the credit is one of the strangest commodity markets in the country.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Clay Thompson bought 400 acres near the Ocklawaha River in Polk County, Florida, spent several years getting the regulatory permits, and only then began selling what the ground had become: credits. Each credit is a unit of restored wetland function, sold to a developer who needs to offset a wetland filled somewhere else in the same watershed. Thompson's Missing Link Mitigation Bank was one of just two banks approved in Florida in 2022, in a state where credits were already running short in the fastest-growing counties and prices had spiked to record levels. The farmer's field he bought is now inventory in a market most Americans have never heard of.

The market exists because the Clean Water Act says a developer who destroys a wetland must replace it, and the 2008 Compensatory Mitigation Rule says the preferred way to replace it is to buy from a bank. A bank is a restored, created, or preserved wetland whose ecological gains are measured, verified by an interagency review team, and released as credits in stages as the site meets performance standards. The developer buys the credits, the Corps debits them from the bank's ledger in RIBITS — the federal Regulatory In-lieu Fee and Bank Information Tracking System — and the permit moves forward. The bank takes the risk of restoration; the developer takes the certainty of a priced offset.

The scale is larger than the obscurity suggests. RIBITS records 5,151 bank and in-lieu fee sites nationally, of which 3,344 are mitigation banks, and only 2,195 of all sites are approved and report available credits. Over the decade from 2016 to 2025, the ledger recorded 38,734 credit sales — 31,633 from commercial mitigation banks, 4,112 from in-lieu fee programs, and 2,989 at single-client banks built for their own sponsors' projects. The operators are consolidating: Resource Environmental Solutions runs 215 banks across 20 states, with 148 currently listing available credits and 22,775 wetland credits on its books; The Nature Conservancy runs 177 banks across eight states. In Georgia, Resource Environmental Solutions and Ecosystem Investment Partners formed a joint venture to acquire and develop banks serving the Atlanta metro area, with Resource Environmental Solutions as operator and long-term steward.

Florida shows what the market looks like at full size. The state has 131 mitigation banks covering 227,000 acres, ranging from a couple of hundred acres to the 22,000-acre Farmton Mitigation Bank in Volusia and Brevard counties, owned by a Chicago-based investment firm, and the 13,249-acre Everglades Mitigation Bank, owned by Florida Power & Light. Wetlands cover roughly 29 percent of Florida's land, more than any other state. When credits run short there, the shortage is not abstract: state Representative Toby Overdorf told regulators his committee was hearing about a shortage of credits throughout the state, and 30 new banks were in various stages of permitting, with no clear date for when any of them would begin selling.

Price is the part of this market nobody publishes. No mitigation bank posts a price list; credits change hands in private bilateral deals, and the federal registry has no price field. What can be documented is the in-lieu fee alternative, where programs do publish rates: the median published wetland credit across 110 rates from 14 programs is $100,000, with a low of $60,000 and a high of $1,800,000. The Government Accountability Office's 2024 report put the range wider still, finding credit prices from under $100,000 in rural watersheds to over $3 million in metropolitan and coastal regions, driven largely by land cost. State-level figures fill in the middle: in Iowa, an emergent wetland credit runs $35,000 to $75,000 and a forested wetland credit $45,000 to $105,000, while West Virginia's in-lieu fee program charges $127,500 per wetland credit. A developer who needs ten credits in a coastal watershed can be looking at a seven-figure line item before construction starts.

The case against the whole arrangement is that a credit is a fiction with a price tag. A restored wetland is not the wetland that was filled: the hydrology differs, the species differ, the location differs, and the replacement takes years to mature while the loss is immediate. Critics point out that banks release credits in stages before the site has fully proven itself, and that the "no net loss" goal the system is meant to serve has not been met — the country lost a net 221,000 acres of wetlands between 2009 and 2019. If the commodity being sold does not actually replace what was destroyed, the market is an accounting exercise that licenses destruction at a discount.

The defense is comparative, not absolute. Before banking, a developer built a small replacement wetland beside the project — a postage-stamp marsh, often poorly built and rarely monitored, that failed at high rates. A bank consolidates the work into a large site, professionally built and monitored for years, with a permanent steward and a financial assurance behind it. Wisconsin's biennial report makes the arithmetic concrete: in 2023 and 2024, the state approved 116 wetland permits that required the purchase of more than 163 mitigation credits, while 11 new banks added 1,438 acres of restoration and generated 700 new credits; since 2012, the state's program has restored 5,225 acres and generated 2,632 credits, with 81 percent of requirements met by private banks. The system is imperfect, and the alternative it replaced was worse.

That is why the market keeps growing even as its critics keep writing. The U.S. Department of Agriculture committed $4.4 million in November 2026 to seven new wetland mitigation banking projects for farmers, on top of 41 projects funded since 2016. One market-research estimate puts the global mitigation banking market at $9.84 billion in 2024, growing to $36.54 billion by 2034. The credit is a strange commodity — invisible, unpriced in public, tied to a specific watershed — and it is also the mechanism by which the permit system actually functions. Whoever sells the permit, sells the credit next.
