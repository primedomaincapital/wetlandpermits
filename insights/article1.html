The Permit Before the Project

In fiscal year 2023 the Corps issued permits covering impacts to more than 73,000 acres of aquatic resources. Every one of those acres passed through a rulebook most developers never read until it stops them.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The surveyor is knee-deep in cordgrass before the sun is properly up, auger in one hand and a pink flag in the other. The soil core comes up grey with rust-colored mottles — hydric soil, the first of three signs. By noon the flags will mark a line across ground the developer bought as dry upland, and that line will decide whether the warehouse on the drawing board needs a federal permit, a state certification, and a check written to a mitigation bank three counties away. The delineation is the moment a piece of real estate becomes a regulatory question, and it happens on thousands of parcels a year.

The rulebook behind that morning starts with one sentence of the Clean Water Act, passed in 1972: no discharge of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States without a permit. Section 404 hands the permit to the Army Corps of Engineers, whose 38 district offices issue them, while the Environmental Protection Agency writes the guidelines the Corps must follow and holds a veto over individual permits. A second section, 401, gives the state a separate say: no federal permit is valid until the state certifies that the project will meet state water-quality standards. A developer needs both, in sequence, before a shovel moves.

Whether the ground counts as a wetland at all turns on a manual older than most of the people who use it. The Corps' 1987 Wetlands Delineation Manual, with its regional supplements, tests three things: wetland hydrology, hydric soils, and hydrophytic vegetation. All three must be present, in the ordinary case, for the ground to be a wetland. The test is fieldwork, not paperwork — soil pits, plant lists, water marks — and it is the reason the first professional a developer hires is usually a wetland scientist rather than a lawyer. Firms such as W3, raSmith, and S&ME sell the delineation as a lead service precisely because it is the step that cannot be skipped or argued around.

Once the line is drawn, the permit itself comes in two shapes. The individual permit is the bespoke route: a public notice, a review of alternatives, a finding that no practicable alternative would do less damage, and a decision by the district engineer. The nationwide permit is the standard route: 57 general permits, reissued in 2021, that authorize categories of work with only minimal adverse effects, each valid for no more than five years. On January 7, 2026, the Corps announced it would reissue 56 of the 2021 permits and issue one new one, while dropping Nationwide Permit 56, which covered finfish mariculture. Most small impacts — a road crossing, a utility line, a dock — travel the nationwide route; the big fills travel the individual one, and the difference in time between them can be measured in years.

Then comes the sequence the regulations call the mitigation hierarchy: avoid the impact where practicable, minimize what cannot be avoided, and compensate for the rest. Compensation is where the money changes hands. The 2008 Compensatory Mitigation Rule sets a preference order — buy credits from an approved mitigation bank first, pay an in-lieu fee program second, build the replacement wetland yourself last — and mitigation is typically required once a wetland impact passes a tenth of an acre. In fiscal year 2023 alone, the Corps issued permits for permanent and temporary impacts to more than 73,000 acres of aquatic resources, according to the Government Accountability Office's September 2024 report. That is the annual volume of ground that passed through this rulebook in a single year.

The case against the rulebook as it stands is easy to make, and developers make it often: it is slow, it is expensive, and its central question — where the wetland ends — is answered by a field test that two competent scientists can read differently on the same parcel. The Corps' own oversight has been uneven; a GAO report published in April 2026, after a review of 85 permit files from three districts covering fiscal years 2017 through 2022, found the Corps had improved its oversight of compensatory mitigation since 2005 but still needed implementation guidance to make that oversight consistent. A permit that depends on which district office answers the phone is a permit whose cost a developer cannot forecast.

The counterweight is what the rulebook protects, and the numbers behind it are stark. The Fish and Wildlife Service counted 116.4 million acres of wetlands in the conterminous United States in 2019 — less than 6 percent of the land — and the country lost a net 221,000 acres of them between 2009 and 2019, with vegetated wetlands down 670,000 acres. Salt marshes shrank 2 percent, the largest percentage loss of any category; freshwater forested wetlands lost 426,000 acres, the largest loss by area. A permitting system that slows a warehouse by a year looks different when the ground it guards is disappearing at that rate, and when more than half of the nation's wetlands outside Alaska and Hawaii have already been lost since European settlement.

That tension — a slow, field-tested gate in front of a shrinking resource — is what makes the service valuable. The delineation, the application, the mitigation purchase: each step needs a specialist who has done it in that district, with that regulator, before. The firms that own the vocabulary of the work win the work, and the vocabulary starts with the two words on this domain.
