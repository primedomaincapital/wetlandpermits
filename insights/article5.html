How Long a Wetland Permit Takes After Sackett

A nationwide permit can verify in 60 days. An individual permit takes six to twelve months. After Sackett, the longest wait is usually the state's one-year certification clock.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The application went in during the spring, complete, with a delineation the Corps had already verified. The developer's lender asked for a date. The consultant said eighteen months, maybe. The lender asked what "maybe" meant, and the consultant said it meant the Corps, the state, and the question of whether the ground was a federal wetland at all — three clocks, running at different speeds, none of them under the developer's control. The loan closed anyway, with a rate lock that expired before the permit arrived. That is the ordinary shape of a Section 404 permit after Sackett: not a single wait, but a sequence of waits, and the longest one is rarely the one the applicant is watching.

The honest answer to how long a wetland permit takes is a range so wide it sounds like evasion. A nationwide permit verification can arrive in about 60 days. A standard individual permit takes six to twelve months, or longer, according to the Corps' own Seattle District guidance — and that is after the application is complete, which is itself a process. The spread is not a failure of the system. It is the system. The permit is five decisions stacked in a row, and the total is the sum of the slowest ones.

Step one: is it a federal wetland at all

Before any permit clock starts, somebody has to decide whether the Clean Water Act reaches the ground. That is the jurisdictional determination, and Sackett v. EPA, decided May 25, 2023, rewrote it. The Supreme Court held that "waters of the United States" covers only relatively permanent, standing or continuously flowing bodies of water — streams, oceans, rivers, and lakes in ordinary parlance — plus wetlands with a continuous surface connection to them, so that the wetland is as a practical matter indistinguishable from the water. The significant-nexus test, which had let the agencies claim jurisdiction over wetlands connected by ecology rather than by surface water, is gone.

What followed was not clarity but a split map. As of November 2026, the Environmental Protection Agency's status page says the agencies apply the January 2023 rule, as conformed to Sackett, in 24 states, the District of Columbia, and the territories — and the pre-2015 regulatory regime, read through Sackett, in the other 26 states. The two regimes are different documents with different definitions, and which one governs a parcel depends on which side of a state line it sits on. A developer with projects in two states is working under two rulebooks, and the Corps' approved jurisdictional determinations — the formal, appealable documents that state whether waters of the United States are present — are written under whichever regime applies, as the Corps' own determination memoranda from its New England and Jacksonville districts in 2025 show.

An approved determination is valid for five years unless new information warrants revision. That is the good news: the answer, once obtained, holds. The bad news is the time to get it. The Corps does not publish a processing time for determinations, and the field work behind one — the delineation — takes two to four weeks, with Corps review of the delineation running 30 to 120 days, according to A3 Environmental Consultants' 2026 pricing guide. A developer who skips the determination and proceeds on the assumption that no federal wetland is present is gambling the whole project on a question the Corps answers for free. It is the cheapest certainty in the process, and the one most often skipped in a hurry.

Step two: the permit the project fits — or doesn't

Once jurisdiction is settled, the permit itself comes in two shapes, and the shape decides the calendar. The nationwide permit is the standard route: 57 general permits, reissued in 2021, authorizing categories of work with only minimal adverse effects, each valid for no more than five years. The current set runs from March 15, 2026 to March 15, 2031, after the Corps announced on January 7, 2026 that it would reissue 56 of the 2021 permits, issue one new one, and drop Nationwide Permit 56, which covered finfish mariculture. The Corps' Seattle District sets a goal of 60 days to process a nationwide permit after receipt of a complete application. Where a pre-construction notification is required, the Corps has 30 days to call it complete, and the applicant can generally proceed 45 days after a complete notification without a reply.

The individual permit is the bespoke route, and its calendar is a different animal. The Corps' Savannah District says two to three months is normally required to process a routine application involving a public notice, and that most applications involving public notices are completed within four months, many within 60 days. Seattle sets a goal of 120 days for a standard individual permit after a complete application — and then adds, in the same guidance, that processing time for individual permits ranges from six to twelve months, or longer, depending on the complexity of the project. The gap between the goal and the range is the public notice, the alternatives analysis, the consultation with other agencies, and the comments that require the application to be changed and re-noticed. The University of Maryland's agricultural risk guide puts it at approximately 45 to 90 days for simpler applications, sometimes longer for complex projects. None of these numbers is wrong. They describe different projects.

Step three: the state review, where the clock can run a year

The step that now takes longest is usually not the Corps at all. It is the state's Section 401 water quality certification, without which no federal permit is valid. The statute gives the certifying authority a "reasonable period of time (which shall not exceed one year)" to act. The Environmental Protection Agency's 2023 rule set a default of six months from the date the request for certification is received, with the federal agency and the certifying authority free to agree on a different period up to one year, and automatic extensions for force majeure and for state public-notice procedures.

A year is a long time to wait for a second signature, and the history of the provision shows why the clock matters. The Second Circuit upheld New York's loss of certification authority after the state failed to act within the one-year deadline on a pipeline project, and the Environmental Protection Agency's January 13, 2026 proposal to revise the 2023 rule — announced by Administrator Lee Zeldin — would narrow the scope of review to point-source discharges, standardize when the clock starts, and prohibit the withdraw-and-resubmit tactic that let certifying authorities restart the one-year clock by asking applicants to withdraw and refile. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, for its part, adopted a categorical one-year reasonable period across its licensing proceedings. The direction of travel is toward a hard year. A hard year is still a year.

The 401 clock is also the step the applicant controls least. The Corps' review is a negotiation; the state's certification is a separate sovereign decision, with its own public notice, its own conditions, and its own politics. A project that fits a nationwide permit in 60 days at the Corps can sit for six months waiting on the state. The developer who budgets for the federal timeline and forgets the state one has budgeted for half the wait.

The counterargument: speed is not the only value

There is a case for the slowness, and it is stronger than developers like to admit. The individual permit's public notice and alternatives analysis are the only moments when a project that would fill a wetland is asked, in public, whether it could be built somewhere else. The 401 certification is the only moment when the state that lives with the water gets a vote. A system that issued permits in 30 days would be a system that had stopped asking those questions, and the ground it governs is disappearing: the Fish and Wildlife Service counted a net loss of 221,000 acres of wetlands between 2009 and 2019, with vegetated wetlands down 670,000 acres. Speed that costs a wetland is not efficiency. It is a transfer.

But the counterweight cuts the other way too. A process whose timeline cannot be forecast — 60 days or 12 months, depending on the district, the state, and the comments — is a process that prices small developers out and rewards only the firms large enough to carry land for a year while the clocks run. The Corps' own oversight has been uneven; the Government Accountability Office, reviewing 85 permit files from three districts in April 2026, found the Corps had improved its oversight of compensatory mitigation since 2005 but still needed implementation guidance to make it consistent. A permit that depends on which district office answers the phone is a permit whose cost a developer cannot forecast, and unforecastable cost is its own kind of tax.

The practical answer, after Sackett, is to treat the timeline as a project in itself. Get the jurisdictional determination early — it is free, appealable, and good for five years — and get it before the purchase agreement is signed, not after. Find out which rulebook governs the state: the conformed 2023 rule in 24 states and the territories, the pre-2015 regime elsewhere. Ask the district for its real timeline, not its goal; Seattle's 120-day goal and its six-to-twelve-month range are both published, and the range is the honest one. And assume the state certification will take the full six months, because the statute allows a year and the proposed rule is still a proposal. The permit is not slow because the government is slow. It is slow because five decisions, made by three sovereigns, have to agree — and the agreement that takes longest is the one nobody budgeted for.
