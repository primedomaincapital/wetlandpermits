After Sackett, the Map Was Redrawn in Pencil

The Supreme Court's 2023 Sackett decision replaced the test that had governed wetland jurisdiction for nearly two decades. Three years on, the replacement rule is still being written.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

In 2004, Michael and Chantell Sackett bought a lot near Priest Lake, Idaho, and started backfilling it with dirt and rocks to build a home. The Environmental Protection Agency sent them a compliance order: the lot contained protected wetlands, the fill was a discharge under the Clean Water Act, and they were in violation. The Sacketts had already been to the Supreme Court once, in 2012, winning the right to challenge the order in court at all instead of waiting for the agency to sue them. Nineteen years after they bought the lot, they were back before the Court a second time — and this time the Court did not just decide their case. It rewrote the test for every wetland in the country.

On May 25, 2023, the Court ruled unanimously that the EPA had overreached against the Sacketts, and a five-justice majority went further. Writing for the majority, Justice Samuel Alito — joined by Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Clarence Thomas, Neil Gorsuch, and Amy Coney Barrett — held that the Clean Water Act covers only relatively permanent, standing or continuously flowing bodies of water, and wetlands with a continuous surface connection to those waters, so that it is difficult to tell where the water ends and the wetland begins. A wetland that merely sits near a stream, or affects one through groundwater, or connects only when it floods, is no longer federal water. The decision, Sackett v. EPA, 598 U.S. 651, discarded the test that had governed for nearly two decades.

The discarded test came from Justice Anthony Kennedy's concurrence in Rapanos v. United States in 2006. Under the "significant nexus" standard, a wetland fell under federal jurisdiction if it significantly affected the chemical, physical, or biological integrity of a traditional navigable water — an ecological judgment, made case by case. The Sackett majority called that standard unconstitutionally vague, noting that by the EPA's own admission nearly all waters and wetlands were potentially subject to regulation under it, putting ordinary landowners at risk of criminal prosecution for moving dirt. The agencies had built two decades of permitting practice on Kennedy's single concurrence; the Court removed the foundation in one opinion.

The practical effect was immediate and uneven. On September 8, 2023, the EPA and the Corps published a conforming rule that removed the significant nexus standard from the regulatory text, deleted interstate wetlands as an independent basis for jurisdiction, and redefined "adjacent" to mean having a continuous surface connection. But litigation had already enjoined the agencies' 2023 definition of waters of the United States in 26 states, so the country split: in 24 states and the District of Columbia the agencies apply the amended 2023 rule, while in the other 26 they apply the pre-2015 regulations as constrained by Sackett. The same wetland, in two states, can now be federal in one and not in the other — and the boundary between them is a court injunction, not a river.

The agencies have tried to close the split, and the attempt has become its own story. On November 17, 2025, the EPA and the Corps released a proposed rule to revise the definition of waters of the United States and implement Sackett fully, defining "relatively permanent" to mean flowing year-round or at least during the wet season, and "continuous surface connection" to require surface water touching a jurisdictional water. The proposal was published in the Federal Register on November 20, 2025, drew roughly 220,000 comments by the January 5, 2026 deadline, and then, on September 4, 2026, drew a supplemental notice proposing a narrower alternative: "relatively permanent" would mean perennial water, flowing every day of the year, and the terms "adjacent" and "abutting" would be removed from the regulatory text altogether. Each round narrows the map a little more, and each round restarts the argument about what the Court actually meant.

The dissent from within the Court deserves a hearing, because it frames the risk the majority accepted. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, and Ketanji Brown Jackson, wrote that the majority misread "adjacent": for the forty-five years since the Clean Water Act passed, "adjacent" had meant neighboring, not touching, and the statute's own text treats adjacent wetlands as a distinct category from the waters they sit beside. A wetland separated from a river by a levee or a berm — the very structures built to hold rivers back — can be ecologically inseparable from the river and still fail the new test. The majority's answer was textual: the Act covers "waters," and a wetland is a water only when it is practically indistinguishable from one. The disagreement is not about science; it is about how much weight a single word should carry.

For developers, the decision cut both ways, and the second edge is the one that matters now. Parcels that were jurisdictional wetlands in 2022 are not federal wetlands in 2026, which means no Section 404 permit, no mitigation purchase, and a faster project — in the states where the agencies apply Sackett. But the ground did not stop being wet, and state regulation has moved into the space the federal government left: states with their own wetland programs now decide what the Corps no longer does, and their rules differ state by state, sometimes county by county. A developer who skips the federal permit and fills a state-regulated wetland has traded a slow federal process for a fast state enforcement action. The jurisdictional determination — the formal answer to whether a parcel is federal water — has become the most valuable document in the file, because the answer changes with the rule, the state, and the season.

That is the lasting consequence of Sackett for the permitting trade. The Court did not simplify the rulebook; it replaced a single national test with a set of competing definitions, a split map, and a rulemaking that has now run through three rounds without a final rule. Every parcel near water now needs someone to read the ground, read the rule in that state, and read the direction the next rule is moving — and to put the answer in writing before the fill begins. The map is drawn in pencil. The work of reading it is permanent.
