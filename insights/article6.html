The Mitigation Bank: How Wetland Credits Are Priced — and Who Buys Them

A mitigation bank turns restored wetland into credits, released in stages and priced by watershed — $35,000 in Iowa, $1.8 million in Georgia. The buyer with a fixed site pays the premium.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

The banker walks the field in rubber boots, and the field is not much to look at. It was soybeans five years ago, then the tiles were broken, the ditch was plugged, and the water came back. Now there are sedges where the rows were, and a heron standing in water that was not there when the county plat was drawn. The banker is not admiring the view. He is counting. Every acre that meets its performance standard becomes a credit, and every credit is inventory — priced by the watershed, sold to a developer who needs it, and recorded in a federal ledger. The wetland is the product. The credit is the sale.

Mitigation banking is the strangest market in American real estate: a market where the commodity is ecological function, the price is unpublished, the seller must hold the land forever, and the buyer has no choice about buying. Understanding it means understanding three things — how a credit is made, how it is priced, and who pays the premium — and the answers are less intuitive than the brochures suggest.

How a credit is made

A mitigation bank starts as degraded ground — farmed wetland, drained marsh, a stream channelized into a ditch. The sponsor restores it: re-establishes hydrology, replants native vegetation, and places the land under a conservation easement that runs with the title in perpetuity. The bank is approved by the Corps and the other agencies on an interagency review team, under a mitigation banking instrument that sets the number of credits the bank may sell and the schedule on which they are released. Credits are not sold when the bank is approved. They are released in stages, as the site meets its ecological performance milestones — hydrology, vegetation, survival rates — and withdrawn as they are sold against permitted impacts. A credit that has not been released cannot be sold, no matter what a buyer will pay for it.

Florida's statute states the release schedule plainly. After July 1, 2025, thirty percent of the awarded credits are released when the conservation easement is recorded and the financial assurances are in place; for a preservation-only area, one hundred percent is released on recordation of the easement. The rest comes as the restoration proves itself. The St. Johns River Water Management District defines the unit the same way the federal system does: one mitigation credit is, in general terms, the ecological value gained by the successful creation of one acre of wetlands — measured by functional assessment, not by raw acreage. An acre of high-functioning marsh earns more credit than an acre of marginal ground, and the bank's ledger tracks the total awarded, the number available for sale, and the number used for each impact.

The scale of the system is larger than most developers realize. RIBITS — the Corps' Regulatory In-lieu Fee and Bank Information Tracking System — lists 5,153 bank and in-lieu fee sites nationally. Of those, 3,345 are mitigation banks, and 2,802 of the banks are commercial, built to sell credits to third parties; 449 are single-client banks, built for one owner's own projects. Only 2,195 of the 5,153 sites are approved and report available credits. The rest are proposed, suspended, or sold out. New banks are approved through the year — 106 sites were established in 2025 and 54 so far in 2026 — but approval is slow. In the Corps' Mississippi Valley Division, the median full clock from the start of the process to a bank decision is 564 days, about a year and a half, and the regulator's own clock runs about nine months of that. A bank is a two-year bet before the first credit can be sold.

How a credit is priced

No mitigation bank publishes its credit prices. That sentence, from MitiVision's survey of the market, is the single most important fact about mitigation banking, and it explains why the only published prices are the in-lieu fee rates — the administratively set fees that 23 of the 111 federal in-lieu fee programs publish. Across those published rates, the national median wetland credit is $100,000, with a low of $60,000 and a high of $1,800,000. The Government Accountability Office, in September 2024, put the range wider: under $100,000 per credit in rural watersheds to over $3 million in metropolitan and coastal regions.

The spread is a map of American land values. In Iowa, Snyder & Associates put emergent wetland credits at $35,000 to $75,000 in June 2025, and forested wetland credits at $45,000 to $105,000. In Indiana, credits in the St. Joseph River service area run $120,000 an acre. In Florida, offsetting an acre of average-quality wetland costs about $100,000, and in the Lake Jesup Basin it runs about $220,000 an acre. The Georgia-Alabama Land Trust prices wetland credits at $1,000,000 to $1,800,000 per credit by service area, repriced each quarter. The National Fish and Wildlife Foundation's Sacramento District program prices a credit at around $501,250, with vernal pool credits around $826,250. The price is set by the cost of the land under the bank, the cost of the restoration, the cost of managing the ground forever — Florida requires trust funds for short-term and long-term management — and the scarcity of credits in the watershed where the buyer must buy.

Scarcity is the sharpest edge. A bank may sell only inside its approved service area, usually a watershed, and the credit must match the resource type and accounting method in the permit. When the credits in a service area are gone, the buyer cannot shop the next watershed. Indiana's in-lieu fee program paused sales in two of its eleven service areas while it works through its queue. Wisconsin, in its January 2025 report on the 2023–2024 period, recorded 116 wetland permits requiring more than 163 credits, eleven new banks covering 1,438 acres and generating 700 new credits — and since 2012, 5,225 acres restored and 2,632 credits generated statewide. The arithmetic of a thin market is that a single large project can clear a watershed's supply, and the next buyer pays whatever the next bank asks.

Who buys — and who pays the premium

The buyers are, first, the developers and infrastructure builders whose projects cannot avoid a wetland: highway departments, housing developers, warehouse builders, utilities, and energy companies. The 2008 Compensatory Mitigation Rule's preference order puts bank credits first, ahead of in-lieu fee programs and permittee-responsible mitigation, so a buyer who can find a bank credit is expected to buy it. The premium payers are the ones with the least flexibility: a project on a fixed site, in a metro watershed, with a construction loan whose rate lock is running. A highway interchange cannot move to a cheaper watershed. A warehouse at an interchange cannot either. The price of the credit is the price of the site, and the site was chosen before the credit was priced.

The second class of buyer is the government itself. State transportation departments are among the largest credit purchasers in the country, and their demand is lumpy: a single highway project can require hundreds of credits, and the bank that serves that watershed sells out in a week. The third is the mitigation banker as buyer: firms such as Resource Environmental Solutions, which operates 215 banks across 20 states with about 22,775 wetland credits available, and The Nature Conservancy, with 177 banks across eight states, buy and consolidate bank assets — RES and Ecosystem Investment Partners acquired eleven Georgia banking assets from Mitigation Resource Group in a joint venture — because scale is the only defense against a market where each watershed is its own monopoly.

The sellers, meanwhile, are a mix that would surprise an outsider. Some are specialist firms — Westervelt Ecological Services, The Earth Partners, Ducks Unlimited — that build banks as a business. Some are farmers and timber owners who discovered that a drained field is worth more as a bank than as cropland. Some are utilities: the Everglades Mitigation Bank, at 13,249 acres, belongs to Florida Power & Light, and the Farmton Mitigation Bank covers 22,000 acres. Florida has 131 mitigation banks covering 227,000 acres, and roughly 29 percent of the state's land is wetland — the bank is not a niche in Florida. It is a land use.

The case against the bank

The case against mitigation banking is serious, and it comes from ecologists as often as from developers. A credit is an abstraction — a unit of functional value — and the question of whether a restored acre in one watershed truly replaces a filled acre in another is a scientific argument, not a settled fact. The bank is often miles from the impact, in a different part of the watershed, serving different wildlife. The developer pays, the wetland is filled, and the replacement is somewhere the people who lost the wetland will never see. The Government Accountability Office's April 2026 review of 85 permit files from three districts found that the Corps had improved its oversight of compensatory mitigation since 2005 but still needed implementation guidance to make that oversight consistent — a polite way of saying that whether a bank's credits are real depends on which district is watching.

There is also the price problem. A market with no published prices, where the buyer must buy and the seller knows it, is a market that invites the suspicion of gouging. Maryland's environmental agency, weighing higher in-lieu fee rates in August 2025, asked directly how its proposed regulations addressed price gouging by mitigation bankers. The answer, in practice, is that the in-lieu fee rate acts as a ceiling of sorts: a bank that prices far above the program's published fee will lose buyers to the program, where the program has credits to sell. Where the program has no credits — Indiana's paused service areas, for instance — the ceiling disappears.

Both criticisms are fair, and neither is fatal. The bank system exists because the alternative — every developer restoring its own wetland, monitored by a Corps district that cannot watch them all — failed more often. The credit is a fiction, but it is a fiction with a ledger, a conservation easement, a trust fund, and a federal database tracking every release and withdrawal. The buyer who pays $220,000 an acre in the Lake Jesup Basin is not buying a wetland. He is buying the certainty that the Corps will accept the compensation, that the ground will be managed forever, and that the permit will issue. In a market where the alternative is a two-to-eight-year approval for a bank of your own, that certainty is the product — and the price is whatever the watershed will bear.
