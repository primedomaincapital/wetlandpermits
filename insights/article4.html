What a Wetland Permit Costs: Delineation, Mitigation, and the Credit Price

A delineation costs $3,500 to $8,000 and the permit fee is smaller still. The mitigation credit — $35,000 in Iowa, $1.8 million in Georgia — is the bill that decides the project.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

The developer outside Chicago had already paid for the land. Forty acres of flat ground near an interchange, priced for a warehouse, closed in March. The wetland consultant arrived in April, walked the back twelve acres with an auger, and found hydric soil under ground the seller had called dry. The quote that followed was the first of three bills the project would pay before a shovel moved: about $3,500 to $8,000 for the delineation alone, according to the 2026 pricing published by A3 Environmental Consultants, an Illinois firm that sells the work. The developer had budgeted for concrete and steel. He had not budgeted for the ground itself to cost money twice — once to buy, once to prove what it was.

That is the shape of the wetland permitting bill. It arrives in three parts, in a fixed order, and the part that looks like the main event is the cheapest. The delineation is a few thousand dollars. The permit application costs almost nothing at the federal counter. The mitigation credit — the payment that replaces the wetland the project fills — is where a five-figure project becomes a six- or seven-figure one. Anyone who prices a deal on the cost of the permit has priced the wrong thing.

The delineation: a few thousand dollars, and the cheapest surprise

A3 Environmental Consultants breaks the work into four lines. A desktop screen of maps, soils data, and the National Wetlands Inventory runs about $1,000. The field survey — the auger, the flags, the plant lists, the GPS mapping — runs $1,000 to $3,000 or more. The written report the Army Corps of Engineers will review runs $1,000 to $2,000 or more. The permit application and agency coordination runs $600 to $1,500 or more. The total for a standard project in Illinois: approximately $3,500 at the low end, $8,000 or more at the high end, in the firm's 2026 pricing. A typical 5-to-10-acre site takes about a day in the field, and the whole delineation takes two to four weeks from start to finished report.

Other firms price the same work differently, and the spread is a warning about comparing quotes. Transect, a software vendor, published a 2023 guide putting a delineation at $700 to $850 for a project under five acres and $15,000 to $25,000 on larger sites. The two firms are not pricing the same scope — one quotes a standard parcel with a report ready for Corps review, the other a range that runs from a small lot to a large tract — and a developer who shops on the headline number will buy the wrong service. The question to ask is what the price includes: the desktop screen, the field work, the report, the Corps verification request, and the state review are separate lines, and leaving one out moves the cost to a later invoice rather than removing it.

One line on the bill is zero, and it matters. The Corps charges no fee to verify a delineation or to issue a jurisdictional determination, the document that states whether waters of the United States are present on a parcel. Cypress Environmental, a firm working across Mississippi, Alabama, Florida, and Louisiana, states it plainly: the agency fees in a project usually come later, with the permit. The delineation is a private-sector cost. The federal government reviews it for free.

The permit fee is the smallest line on the page

State fees are where the public charges begin, and they are modest beside what follows. Wisconsin's Department of Natural Resources charges $300 per acre to identify wetlands on up to five acres, $300 per 20 acres to confirm a consultant's delineation, and a $500 application processing fee under state statute. Oregon's Department of State Lands, on its 2026 schedule, charges $600 to review a delineation report covering 0.2 wetland acres or less, $1,200 above that, and $3,500 when the study area also exceeds 100 acres in a west-side county or 200 acres in an east-side county. These are real numbers, and they are the kind of numbers a developer can carry in a spreadsheet without flinching. None of them is the bill that stops a project.

The reason is arithmetic. A delineation at $8,000 and a state review at $3,500 are rounding errors on a warehouse that costs millions. They are also fixed costs: they do not grow with the size of the wetland impact. The next line does. It grows with every tenth of an acre, and it grows at a price set by a market the developer does not control.

The credit is the bill

Mitigation is typically required once a wetland impact passes a tenth of an acre, under the 2008 Compensatory Mitigation Rule, and the rule's preference order puts buying credits from an approved mitigation bank first. The price of those credits is the most opaque number in American environmental regulation, because no mitigation bank publishes its prices. The only published prices in the market are the in-lieu fee rates — the administratively set fees that 23 of the 111 federal in-lieu fee programs publish — and they show a spread wide enough to change the economics of a site.

The Government Accountability Office, in its September 2024 report on the costs of compensatory mitigation, put the range at under $100,000 per credit in rural watersheds to over $3 million in metropolitan and coastal regions. MitiVision, which mirrors the Corps' RIBITS registry, counts a national median of $100,000 per published wetland credit, with a low of $60,000 and a high of $1,800,000 across the programs that publish rates. The Georgia-Alabama Land Trust prices wetland credits at $1,000,000 to $1,800,000 per credit by service area, repriced each quarter. The National Fish and Wildlife Foundation's Sacramento District California program prices a credit at around $501,250, with vernal pool credits around $826,250. Maine charges $187,744 to $250,034 per acre. Pennsylvania's program runs $118,000 to $158,400 per acre. The Nature Conservancy's Virginia program charges $60,000 to $250,000 per acre. In Florida, a credit runs about $78,000 on the state's functional assessment, and the average cost of offsetting an acre of average-quality wetland is about $100,000 — except in the Lake Jesup Basin near Winter Springs, where it runs about $220,000 an acre, the most expensive in the country. In Indiana, credits in the St. Joseph River service area run $120,000 an acre, and the state's in-lieu fee program has collected at least $109 million since 2018. In Iowa, Snyder & Associates put emergent wetland credits at $35,000 to $75,000 in June 2025, and forested wetland credits at $45,000 to $105,000.

Read that list twice and the pattern is plain. The same tenth of an acre costs $35,000 in an Iowa corn county and $1,800,000 in a Georgia metro service area. The credit is not priced by the wetland. It is priced by the ground around it — by land values, by how many banks serve the watershed, and by how much development is competing for the same credits. A developer who buys land first and prices the credit second has bought the land blind.

Why one credit costs ten times another

Three forces set the price. The first is land. A bank is restored wetland on ground somebody bought, placed under a conservation easement, and committed to manage forever; in a metro county, the land alone costs more than the restoration. The second is the replacement ratio. Compensation is rarely one credit per acre of impact — ratios run higher than one to one, so a two-acre fill can require three or four credits, and every credit is priced at the regional rate. The third is scarcity inside a service area. A bank may sell only inside its approved watershed, and when the credits in that watershed are gone, there is no substitute at any price. Indiana's program paused credit sales in two of its eleven service areas, including Upper White, which covers the Indianapolis metro, while it works through its queue. The price in the next watershed over is irrelevant. The developer's project is in this one.

The in-lieu fee alternative does not escape the same math. The fee is set to cover the full cost of the replacement project the program will eventually build, plus administration, and in several states it is the more expensive route: Maryland's environmental agency, weighing higher in-lieu fee rates in August 2025, noted that West Virginia's $60,000-per-credit rate was old and no longer enough to do the work. A fee that is too low produces a program that cannot build; a fee that is high enough produces a bill the developer resents. Either way, the credit — or the fee that stands in for it — is the number that decides whether the project pencils out.

The case for paying it anyway

There is a fair objection, and developers make it with feeling: the price is set by a market with no published prices, no competing quote the buyer can check, and a seller who knows the buyer has no alternative. That is a description of a monopoly, and in a single watershed it often is one. The counterweight is the alternative. Building the replacement wetland yourself — permittee-responsible mitigation — means buying the land, doing the restoration, and monitoring it for years, with the Corps holding the permit hostage to performance standards the developer has never met before. Getting a mitigation bank approved takes about two to eight years in Florida, including a conservation easement and trust funds for short-term and long-term management. The bank exists precisely so that a developer who has never restored a wetland does not have to learn how.

Scale is also on the buyer's side, in a limited way. Resource Environmental Solutions operates 215 banks across 20 states with about 22,775 wetland credits available, and The Nature Conservancy operates 177 banks across eight states. The market is not a single seller; it is a thin one, and thin markets price badly. The honest statement is that the credit system trades one risk for another: the developer gives up price transparency and gains certainty that the compensation exists, is approved, and will be accepted by the Corps.

That trade is worth making only if it is priced before the land is bought. The delineation at $3,500 to $8,000 is the cheapest information in the deal. The credit quote — obtained from the bank that serves the watershed, before the purchase agreement is signed — is the most expensive, and the one most often skipped. A warehouse that needs four credits at $120,000 an acre in the St. Joseph service area carries a $480,000 line the pro forma never showed. A site with the same footprint in an Iowa watershed carries $140,000 to $300,000. The difference is not the building. It is the ground under it, priced by a market that publishes almost nothing and forgives nothing.
